## Start with a place, not an idea

Walk the street you already walk. Find the spot people pass through but never stop in: a wide pavement, a courtyard, the bit of grass nobody mows. That spot is the idea. What you do there matters less than the fact that somebody stopped.

## Ask three people

Not thirty. Three people who live within five minutes and would notice if it did not happen. If you cannot find three, the place is wrong, not the plan.

## Make it about food or work

People need a reason to stand next to a stranger for an hour. Cooking gives them one. So does carrying something heavy, or building a thing that needs four hands. Anything where the task does the talking for you.

## Say a time, not a programme

“Thursday from six, at the corner” is enough. A programme invites an audience; a time invites participants. Nobody should have to know what will happen in order to come.

## Plan for twice as many and half as much

Twice as many people as you expect, half as much of everything you thought you needed. It has never once gone the other way.

## Let the first one go wrong

The first attempt is the invitation to the second. Failing in front of forty people is a faster way to make friends than succeeding on your own — nobody remembers the popcorn that worked.

## Ask permission afterwards, unless you cannot

Most public space tolerates people enjoying it. Some does not: if you need a permit, ask early, keep it short, say how many people and how long, and offer to clean up. It is usually one form and one week.

## Write down who came

Names on the back of a receipt is a perfectly good archive. Those names are the second event, and the third.
